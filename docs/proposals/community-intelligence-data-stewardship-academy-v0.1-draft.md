## 1. Executive recommendation

**The opportunity.** GFA's volunteers, peer supporters, navigators, staff, and leaders all touch information about people — referrals, follow-ups, resource lists, aggregate results. The organization already has an unusually strong governance spine for a small nonprofit: a ratified Relational Operating Framework, an Institutional Evidence Ledger (Classes A–F), a Knowledge Authority Protocol, and a current Language Guide (all read against the Drive originals for this draft; see Section 9). What it does not yet have is a simple, teachable way to put that discipline into the hands of the people doing frontline and administrative work. A Community Intelligence & Data Stewardship Academy — a small, practical learning path hosted in the knowledge center of GraceForAddictions.org — can close that gap: it turns governance documents few people will read into habits many people can practice.

**Intended audiences.** Level 1 for volunteers, peer supporters, navigators, and new staff; Levels 2–4 progressively for coordinators, program leaders, development staff, directors, and board members. No learner needs every level.

**Strongest improvements to the original concept.**

1. **Governance is built into the lessons, not stapled on.** Every module teaches the actual GFA distinctions (referral ≠ connection; activity ≠ outcome; evidence class ≠ status label) instead of generic nonprofit data advice.
2. **Synthetic data only.** All exercises use clearly labeled fictional data. No real participant details ever enter teaching materials.
3. **Honest claims throughout.** The earlier material's unverified elements were verified or labeled before use: the Recovery Iowa attribution was **confirmed against the live public page** (recovery-iowa.org/data-management-and-smartsheet/, read 2026-11-10); the "4–6 hours" duration, numerical quality ratings, and readiness claims are removed or replaced with labeled proposals. The external-resource inventory reports exactly what was and was not inspected.
4. **Recognition demoted to an open question.** The proposed completion labels ("Community Steward," etc.) are optional internal acknowledgments at most — never credentials — and my recommendation (below) is to defer them entirely for the pilot.
5. **Burden is a design constraint.** Each module and metric carries an explicit helper-burden consideration, consistent with the doctrine that documentation serves the relationship.

**Recommended minimum viable pilot (Proposed).** Level 1 only: six short facilitated modules using existing tools (Google Workspace documents and spreadsheets GFA already uses — no new software purchase), one synthetic dataset, one capstone, a small cohort (Proposed: 5–8 learners mixing volunteers and staff), and structured learner/facilitator feedback. Assumptions: one facilitator can be designated; learners can give roughly 1–1.5 hours per module (Proposed, unvalidated); materials delivered as print-friendly documents.

**Defer.** Levels 2–4 full builds (blueprint only now); any learning-platform or Smartsheet subscription decision; recognition labels; the knowledge graph, statewide resource network, automated narrative engine, and new grant portal (see "Do not build yet," Section 8); any public "enroll now" website language until a launch decision exists.

**What existing capability supports this.** Supported by sources read for this draft: the Governance Framework Bundle v1.0 supplies the evidence ladder, Evidence Ledger classes, and conflict-resolution rules the curriculum teaches; the Language Guide (Oct 21, 2026 edition) supplies the voice and terminology standards; the Grant Readiness Master Register demonstrates a live, maintained verification register the academy can point to as a worked example of the practices it teaches (without duplicating it). No claim is made here about staffing, funding, platform capability, or operational readiness beyond these documents.

**This is a proposal for review**, not an announcement. The decisions it needs are listed at the end of Section 8.

## 3. Academy blueprint

Design rules across all levels: no learner must complete every level; each level states who it serves; prerequisites are minimal; every module has an accessible alternative (print packet + discussion in place of screen work); durations and assessment thresholds are **Proposed and unvalidated**.

### Level 1 — Community Data Foundations *(pilot: essential)*

- **Audience:** volunteers, peer supporters, navigators, new staff.
- **Prerequisites:** none.
- **Practical competencies:** write a factual, dignified documentation note; recognize what information not to collect or share; clean a small dataset; tell an attempted referral from a completed connection; classify evidence honestly; read a simple aggregate dashboard without over-claiming.
- **Modules:** the six in Section 4.
- **Format and time (Proposed):** six facilitated sessions of 45–60 minutes plus ~15–30 minutes practice each; also workable as three 2-hour blocks. Total ~6–8 hours. (The earlier "4–6 hours" figure was not validated; neither is this one — the pilot measures actual time.)
- **Assessment:** completion of each module's practice activity plus the capstone, reviewed against the rubric in Section 4. No percentage pass mark is proposed; the rubric is criterion-based.
- **Accessible alternatives:** print packets, verbal walk-throughs of the dataset exercise, large-print dataset sheets, no timed elements.
- **Optional recognition:** see recommendation below.

### Level 2 — Resource & Referral Management *(later development; outline only)*

- **Audience:** navigators, circle leaders, resource coordinators. **Prerequisite:** Level 1 or equivalent demonstrated practice.
- **Competencies:** verify a resource before listing it (contact, eligibility, cost, last-verified date); run a referral workflow that preserves participant choice; document follow-through dispositions (completed, pending, declined, unreachable, withdrawn) accurately; maintain a resource directory without creating a duplicate tracker; coordinate with partners without over-sharing.
- **Module outline (Proposed):** (1) Resource verification and freshness; (2) Referral workflows and participant choice; (3) Follow-through and dispositions; (4) Partner coordination and information boundaries.
- **Assessment:** scenario walkthroughs using synthetic resource/referral cases.

### Level 3 — Impact & Reporting *(later development; outline only)*

- **Audience:** program leaders, staff, development team. **Prerequisite:** Level 1; Level 2 recommended for program staff.
- **Competencies:** read and apply a metric definition (numerator, denominator, cohort, period, exclusions); build an honest aggregate chart; distinguish targets from results; prepare grant evidence with provenance; use authorized storytelling rules (consent, optionality, no story-to-metric conversion); interpret uncertainty and small numbers.
- **Module outline (Proposed):** (1) Metric definitions and the evidence ladder in reporting; (2) Dashboards and honest visualization; (3) Grant evidence and the readiness register; (4) Authorized storytelling; (5) Uncertainty, small cells, and what not to publish.
- **Assessment:** produce one fully specified metric definition and one defensible aggregate summary from synthetic data.

### Level 4 — Organizational Intelligence *(later development; outline only)*

- **Audience:** directors, board members, designated leaders. **Prerequisite:** Level 1; Level 3 recommended.
- **Competencies:** use evidence classes and the authority hierarchy in decisions; evaluate a program proposal's measurement plan; weigh burden against information value; plan sustainably (planning cadence, goal evidence); govern data access and retention questions by asking the right questions rather than inventing policy ad hoc.
- **Module outline (Proposed):** (1) Decision authority vs. factual authority; (2) Program evaluation basics; (3) Resource allocation and burden; (4) Community mapping and partnership intelligence; (5) Governance and sustainability.
- **Assessment:** case deliberation exercises; no scored test.

### Recognition recommendation

The proposed labels ("Community Steward," "Resource Navigator," "Impact Steward," "Community Intelligence Leader") are **optional internal completion acknowledgments only** — not professional qualifications, accredited credentials, permission to practice, or proof of competence for any regulated role, and the materials must say so wherever a label appears. **Recommendation: defer all four labels past the pilot.** A simple facilitator note of completion costs nothing to administer; named recognitions require tracking, consistency, and expectations management that a small organization should not take on until the curriculum itself has proven useful. Revisit after the pilot with actual completion data. (Proposed; needs an organizational decision either way.)

### Module 1 — Information as Stewardship

**Purpose.** Ground every later skill in why GFA documents at all: information is held in trust for people and the community, and documentation serves the relationship.

**Learning objectives.** The learner can (1) explain in their own words why GFA records information — continuity, agency, safety, accountability, follow-through, verification, learning, required reporting; (2) give one example of documentation that serves a relationship and one that burdens it; (3) state the "minimum sufficient documentation" rule and apply it to a scenario.

**Lesson (learner-facing).** When someone trusts Riverbend — or GFA — with a piece of their story, that trust doesn't end when the conversation does. Good records are how the next helper picks up where the last one left off, how a promise made on Tuesday gets kept on Friday, and how the organization can honestly say what it did and what changed. That's stewardship: the information belongs to the relationship and its purpose, not to whoever happened to type it. Stewardship cuts both ways. Writing too little breaks continuity — the participant has to retell their story, promises get lost. Writing too much creates a different harm: surveillance, embarrassment-in-waiting, and hours a helper spent typing instead of being present. GFA's rule is **minimum sufficient documentation**: enough for continuity, safety, accountability, participant agency, follow-through, verification, learning, and required reporting — and no more. Before you write, ask: *Who needs this later, and for what?* If no honest answer exists, don't record it. People are not scores, records, diagnoses, or fundraising material; a record describes what happened, not who someone is.

**Worked fictional example.** A volunteer spends 40 minutes with a visitor who talks about a difficult custody situation, a job lead, and wanting a ride to Friday's group. Over-documentation: paragraphs of custody detail. Under-documentation: nothing. Minimum sufficient: *"Visit ~40 min. Asked about ride support for Friday group — told them we'd check and call back by Thursday. Mentioned a job lead he's pursuing himself."* The custody story stays in the relationship unless the participant asks for help with it; the promise (callback) is captured so it gets kept.

**Practice activity.** Learners receive three fictional conversation summaries and write a minimum-sufficient note for each, then compare in pairs: what did you keep, drop, and why?

**Facilitator notes / cautions.** Expect the instinct "more detail = more caring." Honor it, then redirect: care shows in follow-through, not volume. Do not let the group debate real participants' situations; redirect any real example to a fictional reframe. Scope caution: this module explains *why*; consent and access rules come in Module 2 — defer those questions rather than improvising answers.

**Knowledge checks.**
1. *A helper writes: "P-210 is manipulative and probably using again." What's wrong, and what could be written instead?* **Answer guidance:** it records judgment and speculation, not events; it reduces a person to a characterization. Instead: the observable facts — e.g., "P-210 asked two staff separately for bus passes on the same day; second request declined per policy." (Echoes the Language Guide: describe the event and stated reason, not an inferred character.)
2. *True or false: if information might be useful someday, we should record it now.* **Answer guidance:** false — "might be useful someday" is the collection rationale GFA doctrine explicitly rejects; record for a present, nameable purpose.

**Completion criterion (Proposed).** The learner's three practice notes each capture commitments and next steps, omit extraneous personal detail, and contain no judgment language, per facilitator review.

### Module 2 — Privacy, Trust & Responsibility

**Purpose.** Teach the practical boundaries: what is never shared, where different kinds of information live, and what participants are entitled to.

**Learning objectives.** The learner can (1) name the categories that stay in approved operational systems only (case notes, consent records, payment details, IDs, sensitive recovery information); (2) explain that participation in services does not imply consent for stories, photos, quotes, or fundraising use; (3) respond correctly to three boundary scenarios (a curious visitor, a well-meaning partner, a social-media moment).

**Lesson (learner-facing).** Trust is the center's real infrastructure, and privacy is how trust survives contact with paperwork. A blanket slogan like "no individual owns community information" is wrong — the truth has parts: **participants have rights** in information about them (to be told why it's collected, to have consent honored where consent applies, to ask for corrections); **the organization is a custodian**, responsible for protecting what it holds and using it only for its purpose; **access follows role and need**, not curiosity or convenience. (Exactly which correction, retention, and deletion processes GFA offers is an organizational decision — this course does not invent them; it teaches you to route such requests to the designated staff contact.) Practical rules you can act on today: sensitive records (case notes, consent records, resident payment details, IDs, recovery details) live only in the approved operational system — never in personal phones, group chats, spreadsheets, or AI tools. Aggregate numbers for planning must be properly de-identified and authorized — and small groups can still identify people ("the only veteran in Tuesday group" is identifiable no matter how anonymous the chart looks). Being served is never consent to be a story: stories, photos, and quotes are optional, separately authorized, and a metric never needs a face to be valid. When in doubt, share less and ask the designated staff contact.

**Worked fictional example.** A local reporter visits Riverbend and asks a volunteer, "Can you introduce me to someone with a great recovery story?" Correct response: warm, firm, routed — "I can't connect you with participants, but our director can talk with you about the center and about how we handle stories." No participant is pointed out, even flatteringly.

**Practice activity.** Three scenario cards (visitor asks who someone is; partner agency emails asking for "a list of everyone you've referred to us"; a celebration moment someone wants to photograph). Learners write or role-play the response, naming *what* they protect and *where* they route the request.

**Facilitator notes / cautions.** Do not state specific legal obligations (e.g., HIPAA or 42 CFR Part 2 applicability) — whether and how those apply to specific GFA records is outside this course's authority; say "the designated staff contact and current policy govern that." Do not promise confidentiality beyond actual policy and system capability. If learners ask about GFA's deletion or correction process, record the question for leadership rather than answering.

**Knowledge checks.**
1. *A funder asks for "a few quotes from people you've helped" by Friday. The program has consent forms for two participants covering fundraising use from last year. What do you check before sending anything?* **Answer guidance:** that the consent actually covers this use and is current; that the person would still be comfortable (consent can be withdrawn); that aggregate findings could serve instead; and that the designated staff contact approves the release. Deadline pressure changes nothing.
2. *Why can a bar chart of "participants by housing status, Tuesday group (n=6)" be a privacy problem even with no names?* **Answer guidance:** small-group disclosure — with six people, categories can identify individuals to anyone who knows the group. Suppression/aggregation rules exist for this reason (the specific threshold is a proposed organizational decision, not a number to invent).

**Completion criterion (Proposed).** All three scenario responses protect the information, avoid inventing policy, and route correctly, per facilitator review.

### Module 3 — Data Hygiene Fundamentals

**Purpose.** Hands-on practice finding and fixing the ordinary defects that make records untrustworthy.

**Learning objectives.** The learner can (1) find duplicates, format inconsistencies, missing values, misplaced personal detail, and stale entries in a small dataset; (2) fix what is fixable and flag what is not, without guessing; (3) explain why standardized categories make counting possible.

**Lesson (learner-facing).** Dirty data is rarely dishonest — it's hurried. Two date formats, one event entered twice, a category spelled three ways, a phone number dropped into a notes field: each is small, but together they make every count wrong and every report shaky — and someone downstream will trust those numbers. Hygiene is a cycle, not a one-time rescue: **audit** (look at what's there), **clean** (fix what evidence supports fixing), **standardize** (agree on formats and category words), **maintain** (small regular habits beat heroic purges). One rule above all: **fix only what you can verify; flag what you can't.** An impossible date isn't yours to guess into correctness — guessing manufactures a fact. And hygiene includes privacy: personal details in the wrong field are a hygiene defect with a human cost.

**Worked fictional example.** The facilitator walks R-003/R-007 (duplicate + date format) and R-006 (name and phone in notes) from the Riverbend log, modeling the audit-clean-standardize-flag sequence, including writing a flag note for R-011's impossible date instead of "correcting" it.

**Practice activity.** Learners receive the full 14-row Riverbend log and 20–30 minutes to produce a cleaned copy plus a flag list. Debrief against the answer key. (Printable version: the log as a table with a worksheet.)

**Facilitator notes / cautions.** The answer key above is the authority; if a learner finds a defect not on it, verify together against the definitions — the dataset is fictional, so the definitions page settles disputes. Caution learners that in real systems they fix data only in the system and role authorized to fix it; the exercise teaches the eye, not a license to edit production records.

**Knowledge checks.**
1. *R-011's date reads 2026-13-01. A teammate says "obviously January 13, just fix it." What do you do?* **Answer guidance:** flag, don't guess — it could be 2026-01-13 or 2026-03-01 (the surrounding rows are early March). The fix is to ask the person who recorded it or mark it unresolved. Changing it silently manufactures evidence.
2. *Why is "peer-support circle" in R-014 a problem when "Group" is used elsewhere?* **Answer guidance:** inconsistent category values split one activity across labels, so counts undercount; standardization isn't pedantry, it's what makes aggregation truthful.

**Completion criterion (Proposed).** The learner's cleaned log catches at least the duplicate, the misplaced personal detail, and the impossible date, with the date flagged rather than guessed.

### Module 4 — Evidence & Verification

**Purpose.** Teach GFA's three separate classification vocabularies and the habit of saying how you know what you claim.

**Learning objectives.** The learner can (1) keep three systems separate — development status (Confirmed / Needs verification / Draft / Proposed), source verification state (Verified / Unverified / Unavailable / Stale / Conflict), and the Institutional Evidence Ledger classes A–F; (2) classify six sample claims into Ledger classes; (3) state what to do when sources conflict (name it UNRESOLVED rather than blending).

**Lesson (learner-facing).** "How do we know that?" is the most useful question in this whole course. GFA answers it with the **Institutional Evidence Ledger**: **A** Primary/Legal Authority (the IRS letter itself), **B** Verified Operational Evidence (the system-of-record count), **C** Leadership-Confirmed (the director confirms it; paper not yet in hand), **D** Reported/Historical (an old grant narrative said so), **E** Inferred (a reasonable conclusion, not established), **F** Unknown. These classes describe *evidence strength*. They are not the same as a document's *development status* (a policy can be a polished Draft and still unadopted) and not the same as a *source verification state* (Verified means "we successfully read this source at a stated revision and time" — it does not mean the source is right, ratified, or implemented). Keeping the three vocabularies separate is what lets GFA be both ambitious and honest at once: you can propose boldly (Proposed), read sources carefully (Verified), and still say the claim itself is only Class E. Two more habits: a number without a period, definition, and source is not a usable fact ("77 served" — when? unduplicated? by whose count?). And when two sources disagree, never average or quietly pick the newer one: write **UNRESOLVED — verification required**, name both sources, and say what would settle it. Recency, repetition, detail, and polish do not establish authority; and an executive decision can set direction, but it cannot make an unverified operational claim true.

**Worked fictional example.** Claim: "Riverbend serves 300 people a year." Trace: it appears in a 2024 flyer (Class D). The front-desk log counts 180 unique person-codes in the last 12 months (Class B, with definition and period). The director believes the 300 included event attendees (Class C explanation). Resolution: report the Class B number with its definition; note the discrepancy and its likely cause; mark the flyer figure superseded for current use — don't average to 240, don't keep repeating 300.

**Practice activity.** Six fictional claim cards (an IRS letter, a database count, a director's statement, an old annual report figure, a staff inference, a question nobody can answer). Learners assign Ledger classes, then separately tag two documents with development status and a source read with verification state — demonstrating the three systems don't collapse into each other.

**Facilitator notes / cautions.** This module must not invent new class systems or rename A–F. If learners propose a "simpler" merged scheme, acknowledge the instinct and explain why the separation is the point (each vocabulary answers a different question). Confirm Ledger definitions against the governing source (Governance Framework Bundle v1.0) when printing materials.

**Knowledge checks.**
1. *The board chair says in a meeting that a partnership MOU was renewed. No document is in hand. What class, and what changes when the signed MOU is located?* **Answer guidance:** Class C (Leadership-Confirmed) now; the located, executed MOU is Class A for the fact of the agreement. Until then, external use should be qualified.
2. *A beautifully formatted dashboard says one thing; a messy spreadsheet of raw entries says another. Which wins?* **Answer guidance:** neither automatically — polish is not authority. Trace both to their sources; the system-of-record evidence (Class B) outranks a derived display. If the conflict can't be traced, it's UNRESOLVED.

**Completion criterion (Proposed).** At least five of six claim cards classified correctly with a stated reason, and the conflict scenario answered with "unresolved + verification path" rather than a guess.

### Module 5 — Introduction to Community Impact

**Purpose.** Teach the evidence ladder — activity to sustained outcome — and the governed meaning of "connection."

**Learning objectives.** The learner can (1) order and define Activity → Engagement → Connection → Progress → Outcome → Sustained Outcome; (2) explain why a referral sent, voicemail, link, appointment, or successful automation is not a completed human connection; (3) compute a simple cohort connection measure with every disposition accounted for.

**Lesson (learner-facing).** GFA's doctrine — "Connection Prevents Crisis" — is a commitment and a *testable hypothesis*, not a proven causal law and not a guarantee. Taking it seriously means measuring honestly. The ladder: an **activity** happened (referral sent); **engagement** means the person is participating; **connection** — the measure GFA treats as its primary organization-level outcome for this design — means a *participant-authorized, completed human connection*: the person chose it, and a real human contact occurred, confirmed by the participant or the receiving person, never inferred from paperwork or automation; **progress**, **outcome**, and **sustained outcome** are further rungs, each needing its own evidence. No rung converts into a higher one by enthusiasm. Counting honestly means a defined **cohort and window**, a numerator **n** (completed, authorized connections), a denominator **N** (everyone eligible in the cohort — *including* people we couldn't reach: **U** unreachable, reported as U/N), and named dispositions for everyone else: pending, declined, withdrawn, unknown. Declined is not failure — it's agency, recorded with respect. If N is zero, the measure is **not applicable** — never 0% or 100%. Also keep *episode counts* (the front desk logged 5 referral episodes) separate from *unique-participant cohorts* (12 of 20 people); merging them double-counts people and is only done through a formal reconciliation, never silently. Eligibility rules, follow-up windows, and reachability protocols are organizational decisions this course does not invent — the exercise states its assumptions as givens.

**Worked fictional example (all numbers fictional, cohort rules given as exercise assumptions).** Riverbend Q2 cohort: N = 20 participants who requested peer connection in the window. Dispositions: completed authorized connections n = 12 (n/N = 60%); unreachable U = 3 (U/N = 15%); pending 2; declined 2; withdrawn 1. Check: 12+3+2+2+1 = 20 ✓. The honest sentence: "Of 20 participants who requested peer connection in Q2, 12 (60%) completed an authorized connection; 3 (15%) could not be reached; 2 were pending at period end; 2 declined; 1 withdrew." The dishonest shortcut the module warns against: "60% success rate" with the unreachable quietly dropped from N (which would inflate to 12/17 = 71%).

**Practice activity.** Learners receive a fictional 15-person disposition list and produce the full accounting (n, N, U, percentages, every disposition, sum check), then write the one honest summary sentence. A second mini-case has N = 0 (answer: not applicable).

**Facilitator notes / cautions.** Watch for two drifts: treating "declined" as a problem to minimize (it's a respected choice) and treating the connection rate as a staff performance score (it is an organizational learning measure; this course never ranks helpers or participants). Do not let the group draft eligibility or follow-up rules "to be helpful" — note such ideas as questions for governance.

**Knowledge checks.**
1. *An automated text went out to 50 participants and 50 were delivered. Someone drafts: "50 connections made." Correct it.* **Answer guidance:** delivery is an activity (a successful automation), not a human connection; none count toward the connection measure until an authorized, completed human contact is confirmed per person.
2. *Why must the 3 unreachable people stay in the denominator?* **Answer guidance:** removing them inflates the rate and hides the follow-up gap; U/N is itself a finding the organization needs to see.

**Completion criterion (Proposed).** The practice accounting sums to N, includes U in the denominator, reports every disposition, and handles the N=0 case as "not applicable."

### Module 6 — Introduction to Dashboards

**Purpose.** Build one small, honest aggregate dashboard from cleaned data, and learn to read dashboards critically.

**Learning objectives.** The learner can (1) build 2–3 simple aggregate views (counts by activity type; referral episodes by disposition) from the cleaned Riverbend log in an approved spreadsheet tool; (2) label a dashboard with its period, definitions, source, and freshness; (3) name three ways a dashboard can mislead (missing denominator, merged measures, implied causation, stale data presented as current).

**Lesson (learner-facing).** A dashboard is a claim wearing a picture. Every honest dashboard answers four questions on its face: *what period? what definitions? what source? when was this last refreshed?* — because a chart with no period is a rumor with an axis. Build small: a count of activities by type and a referral-disposition summary are genuinely useful to a center; a wall of gauges is not. Three integrity rules: show denominators (12 of 20, not a lonely "12"); never let an activity chart sit under an outcome headline; and label freshness by how the data actually updates — a dashboard refreshed monthly is "periodic," and nothing about its looking live makes it live. One privacy rule: dashboards built for the public or for general staff show aggregates only, above the (to-be-decided) suppression threshold; participant-level views belong only in restricted operational systems. And a reading rule for every dashboard you meet: before believing it, find its definitions. If you can't, treat it as unverified.

**Worked fictional example.** From the cleaned log: Activities by type (Visits 3, Groups 2, Calls 1, Referrals 5, Follow-ups 2; period 2026-03-02 to 2026-03-13; source: cleaned Riverbend log v2; refreshed: date of exercise) and Referral episodes by disposition (confirmed 2, declined/deferred 1, unknown/pending 2 — labeled "episode counts, not unique participants"). The facilitator shows the same data presented dishonestly ("83% connection success!" by hiding dispositions) and the group dismantles it.

**Practice activity.** In pairs, learners build the two views in Google Sheets (or on paper grids for the no-screen alternative), add the four labels, and swap for peer critique using a checklist: period? definitions? source? freshness? denominators? episode-vs-person labeling?

**Facilitator notes / cautions.** Keep tooling humble: this exercise requires only the spreadsheet GFA already uses; it establishes no product choice and no "approved data destination" beyond the exercise itself. If learners want conditional formatting and sparklines, fine — but grade the labels, not the decoration. Public-website implications (what may ever be shown publicly) are a leadership decision; the module only teaches the distinction.

**Knowledge checks.**
1. *A dashboard shows "Connections: 47 ▲" with a green arrow. What must you find before repeating that number anywhere?* **Answer guidance:** the definition (connections per the governed measure, or referrals?), the period, the denominator/cohort, the source, and the refresh date. The arrow is persuasion, not evidence.
2. *The center's dashboard hasn't been refreshed since January. It's April. Is the dashboard "wrong"?* **Answer guidance:** it's stale-presented-as-current — the data may be accurate *for its period*; the dishonesty is the missing freshness label. Fix the label (and the refresh habit), not just the numbers.

**Completion criterion (Proposed).** The pair's dashboard carries all four labels, correct counts per the answer key, and the episode-count caveat on the referral view.

## 5. Draft Data Stewardship Principles

**Draft for review — not adopted policy.** Items marked ⚑ need an organizational decision; the principles do not invent those procedures.

1. **Dignity and agency.** People are never reduced to records, scores, diagnoses, or fundraising material; information practice preserves each person's choices. *Practice:* write every record as though the participant may appropriately read it.
2. **Purpose and minimum necessary information.** Collect only what a present, nameable purpose requires. *Practice:* before adding any field or question, write down who needs it later and for what; if no one can, don't collect it.
3. **Rights and responsible custodianship.** Participants have rights in information about them; GFA is a custodian, not an owner, and access follows role, scope, and purpose. *Practice:* route every request about correction, deletion, or access to the designated staff contact ⚑ (the specific rights processes GFA offers require an organizational decision) rather than improvising an answer.
4. **Accuracy and correction.** Records reflect what happened; errors are fixed where evidence supports fixing and flagged where it doesn't. *Practice:* never guess a correction — flag unresolvable defects with a note and a question to the recorder.
5. **Evidence and provenance.** Every material claim carries how-we-know: source, period, definition, and an Evidence Ledger class (A–F). *Practice:* attach "per [source], [period], [definition]" to any number before it leaves a worksheet.
6. **Appropriate access and protection.** Sensitive records live only in approved operational systems; aggregates for planning are authorized and de-identified with small-group risk in mind. *Practice:* keep participant-level information out of personal devices, chats, and AI/planning tools; use only authorized aggregate extracts there ⚑ (suppression threshold to be decided, not assumed).
7. **Simplicity and manageable burden.** A workflow that exhausts helpers degrades both care and data. *Practice:* when adding any documentation step, estimate and record the minutes it costs the helper, and remove or shrink a step elsewhere where possible.
8. **Responsible reporting and optional, authorized storytelling.** Aggregate findings stand on their own; stories are separately consented, optional, and never required to validate a metric. *Practice:* for every public claim, check consent coverage and currency before use, and offer the aggregate-only alternative first.
9. **Continuity, follow-through, and accountability.** Records exist so promises get kept and the next helper can continue the relationship. *Practice:* every note that contains a commitment names the next action and the role responsible for it.
10. **Learning in service of people and community.** Data practice exists to improve support, not to surveil, rank, or impress. *Practice:* close each reporting cycle with one recorded learning question ("what will we do differently?") alongside the numbers — and never produce participant or worker rankings.
